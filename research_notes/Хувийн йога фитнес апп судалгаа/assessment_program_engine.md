## 1. Pre-participation screening (PAR-Q+, red flags, ACSM risk stratification)

### Takeaway
Two complementary, encodable gates exist: the PAR-Q+ (7 yes/no questions → optional follow-up pages → ePARmed-X+ online referral), and the ACSM 2015 algorithm, which drops the old risk-factor counting and decides on only three variables (current regular exercise, known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease, signs/symptoms) plus the intended intensity. Both are designed to reduce unnecessary physician referrals.

### Cited Findings
- The PAR-Q+ starts with 7 yes/no questions. The first three (as reproduced in the result summary) are: (1) "Has your doctor ever said that you have a heart condition? or high blood pressure?"; (2) "Do you feel pain in your chest at rest, during your daily activities of living, OR when you do physical activity?"; (3) "Do you lose balance because of dizziness OR have you lost consciousness in the last 12 months?" — [2025 PAR-Q+ form (Southlake)](https://southlake.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/PARQPlus2025-Form-fillable.pdf); [2025 PAR-Q+ (Jotform copy)](https://form.jotform.com/221436864485060)
- If "yes" to any question on page 1, the person must complete pages 2–3 (follow-up questions about chronic conditions). If "no" to all follow-ups, they are cleared to be physically active. The ePARmed-X+ is the online application used when a follow-up question on pages 2–3 is answered "yes" — [HealthLinkBC: PAR-Q+ and ePARmed-X+](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/node/11457); [BCRPA screening tools](https://healthyliving.bcrpa.bc.ca/fitness-essentials-exercise-screening-tools/)
- Peer-reviewed description of the PAR-Q+ / ePARmed-X+ development — [Warburton et al., PMC3596208](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3596208)
- ACSM formally updated its preparticipation health-screening process in November 2015 (Riebe D, Franklin BA, Thompson PD, Garber CE, Whitfield GP, Magal M, Pescatello LS. MSSE 2015). The algorithm relies on current exercise participation; history/symptoms of cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease; and desired exercise intensity to determine referral status — [ACE: New preparticipation screening guidelines](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/6921/new-preparticipation-screening-guidelines-what-health-and-fitness-pros-need-to-know/); [NFPT: Assessing cardiovascular risk](https://nfpt.com/assessing-cardiovascular-risk-with-fitness-clients/)
- Reason for the change: Whitfield et al. (2014) found that under the previous risk-factor-based standards nearly 95% of adults over 40 would have required a physician visit before any exercise, even walking — [ACE article](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/6921/new-preparticipation-screening-guidelines-what-health-and-fitness-pros-need-to-know/)
- Comparing the 2015 algorithm with the previous version produced a 41% decrease in the number of people who would be referred to a physician before starting exercise; under the new algorithm 2.6% of adults would be referred only before vigorous exercise and 54.2% before any exercise (figures reported in a PMC study applying the algorithm to a population sample) — [PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)
- Universities use the same logic in ethics screening (Carnegie health-screening guidance, Leeds Beckett) — [Leeds Beckett guidance PDF](https://Www.Leedsbeckett.Ac.Uk/-/media/files/student-hub/research-ethics/carnegie-health-screening-guidance.pdf)

### Inferences
- Engine rule: `if any PARQ_1..7 == yes → show follow-up pages; if any follow-up == yes → hard-stop with "medical clearance required (ePARmed-X+ / doctor)"`. The engine should still allow light activity guidance (walking) while clearance is pending, consistent with the 2015 ACSM intent of not blocking light-moderate activity.
- ACSM 2015 branches (from the three variables): no disease + no symptoms → no clearance needed for light-to-vigorous; known disease but asymptomatic and currently exercising → may continue moderate, clearance before progressing to vigorous; known disease and not currently exercising → clearance before starting; any signs/symptoms → clearance regardless. *This branch logic is my reconstruction of the published algorithm; the search results confirmed only the three decision variables, not each branch.* Verify against Riebe 2015 Figure 1.

### Gaps
- Full verbatim text of PAR-Q+ questions 4–7 (medications for a chronic condition; other chronic condition; bone/joint problem aggravated by activity; doctor said only do medically supervised activity) and the 10 follow-up condition categories — eparmedx.com and all mirror PDFs were blocked. Also the PAR-Q+ "delay becoming more active if" clauses (temporary illness, pregnancy → consult ePARmed-X+, blood pressure >160/90) could not be verified.
- ACSM's list of "major signs or symptoms suggestive of CV, metabolic or renal disease" (chest pain, dyspnea at rest/mild exertion, dizziness/syncope, orthopnea/PND, ankle edema, palpitations, claudication, known murmur, unusual fatigue) and the definition of "regular exercise" (≥30 min moderate, ≥3 d/wk, for ≥3 months) — not retrievable; verify in Riebe 2015 / ACSM GETP 11th ed.

## 2. What to ask (goals, experience, time, equipment, injuries, sleep, stress, occupation, female life stage, preferences, motivation) and evidence that these change outcomes

### Takeaway
Of the intake variables, the ones with the clearest outcome evidence are training age (drives volume/periodization defaults), psychological stress (lower strength gains and slower 96-h recovery), chronic sleep restriction (reduced strength-endurance and volume tolerance), and female life stage (pregnancy/postpartum guidance exists; menstrual-cycle phase has only a trivial average effect, so individualize rather than hard-code). Available days and equipment are constraint inputs rather than outcome modifiers.

### Cited Findings
- **Stress:** Bartholomew et al. (2008), 135 undergraduates in 12 weeks of twice-weekly 1.5-h weight training: low-stress participants (Adolescent Perceived Events Scale) had significantly greater increases in 1RM bench press and squat than high-stress participants; social support was unrelated to gains. Conclusion: high life stress may lessen adaptation; coaches should monitor stress outside training — [NASE blog summary](https://www.naseinc.com/blog/effect-of-perceived-stress-on-strength-gains/); [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/strength-gains-after-resistance-training-the-effect-of-stressful-/); [full-text PDF mirror](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/TREINO%20DE%20FOR%C3%87A/Outras%20adapta%C3%A7%C3%B5es/Strength%20gains%20following%20resistance%20training%20-%20the%20effect%20of%20stressful,%20negative%20life%20events.pdf)
- Chronic psychological stress impairs recovery of muscular function and somatic sensations over a 96-hour period after resistance exercise (Stults-Kolehmainen et al.) — [PubMed 24343323](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24343323/); and psychological stress impairs short-term muscular recovery (2012) — [PubMed 22688829](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22688829/)
- **Sleep:** A meta-analysis by Craven et al. found acute sleep loss negatively impacts strength performance but the magnitude is fairly small; sleep loss has a larger negative impact on strength-endurance (e.g., 10RM and volume tolerance) than on 1RM — [Stronger by Science: Research spotlight sleep loss](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-sleep-loss/)
- A systematic review found sleep deprivation had little effect on muscle strength during resistance exercise, whereas consecutive nights of sleep restriction could reduce force output of multi-joint but not single-joint movements; chronic sleep restriction may create an inappropriate hormonal environment for adaptation — [Manipal: Implications of sleep loss on muscle strength (systematic review)](https://researcher.manipal.edu/en/publications/implications-of-sleep-loss-or-sleep-deprivation-on-muscle-strengt/); [Knowles et al., "Inadequate sleep and muscle strength" (Deakin)](https://deakin.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Inadequate_sleep_and_muscle_strength_implications_for_resistance_training/20813233)
- **Menstrual cycle:** McNulty et al. 2020 (Sports Medicine 50(10):1813–1827), 78 studies / 1,193 eumenorrheic women: exercise performance "might be trivially reduced" in the early follicular phase vs all other phases; because of the trivial effect size, high between-study variation and many poor-quality studies, general guidelines cannot be formed and a personalised approach is recommended — [Northumbria repository](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/43759); [RGU record](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/output/940959)
- **Pregnancy/postpartum:** ACOG Committee Opinion 804 (April 2020): pregnant women should aim for at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity weekly, distributed through the week; women who were physically active or did vigorous activity before pregnancy can continue; in the absence of obstetric/medical complications physical activity is safe and desirable; some modification is needed due to anatomic/physiologic changes; benefits include decreased gestational diabetes, cesarean and operative delivery, and shorter postpartum recovery; physical activity can help prevent postpartum depressive disorders — [OPQIC summary of ACOG CO 804](https://opqic.org/acog-committee-opinion-no-804-physical-activity-and-exercise-during-pregnancy-and-the-postpartum-period/); [AMSSM listing](https://learn.amssm.org/AssetListing/Best-Practice-Case-Studies-1088/Exercise-in-Pregnancy-PS-4297)
- **Menopause:** Resistance training in postmenopausal women increases strength, lean mass, reduces fat and benefits bone mineral density; high-intensity protocols (~70–85% 1RM), ~3 sessions/week over ≥6–12 months give the largest gains in lumbar-spine and femoral-neck BMD — [RSD Journal review](https://rsdjournal.org/rsd/article/view/50935); [UCAM 2024 review PDF](https://repositorio.ucam.edu/bitstream/10952/9803/1/2024_Resistance%20training%20effects%20on%20healthy%20postmenopausal%20women.pdf)
- LIFTMOR RCT: high-intensity resistance and impact training improved BMD and physical function in postmenopausal women with osteopenia/osteoporosis; combining resistance with impact/jump loading yields the strongest spinal osteogenic stimulus — [Springer: Osteoporosis Int. article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00198-012-2143-2) (search summary); STOP-EM trial (Canada): twice-weekly progressive heavy resistance at 80–85% 1RM plus drop-landing impact exercise improved total lean mass, leg power and lumbar spine BMD vs control in peri-/early post-menopausal women — [Medznat summary](https://www.medznat.ru/en/science/summaries/exercise-for-bone-and-muscle-health-during-menopau)
- **Training age (why it matters for defaults):** ACSM novice recommendations are 60–70% 1RM, 1–3 sets of 8–12 reps, 2–3 d/wk, with progression of 2–10% load when the trainee can do 1–2 reps over target — [SFU copy of ACSM resistance guidance](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf); [ACSM 2009 Position Stand PDF](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf)

### Inferences
- Encoding: ask goals as a ranked list (max 3) and map to a primary "driver" (strength / hypertrophy / fat loss / mobility / stress / balance). Experience → {novice <6 mo consistent, intermediate 6–24 mo, advanced >2 y} drives starting sets/week, periodization type, and RPE accuracy weighting (see §4).
- Encode pain/injury as `{region: [neck, shoulder_L/R, elbow, wrist, low_back, hip, knee_L/R, ankle_L/R], severity 0–10, aggravating movement pattern: [overhead, deep knee flexion, spinal flexion, spinal extension, impact, loaded hip hinge], cleared_by_clinician: bool}` so exercise-substitution rules can key on movement pattern rather than diagnosis.
- Sleep (<6 h habitual) and perceived stress (e.g., PSS-4 high) should lower the default weekly volume one step and bias toward strength-endurance volume caps rather than 1RM intensity, because the evidence shows strength-endurance/volume tolerance is what suffers.
- Menstrual tracking should be an optional, user-driven modifier (symptom-based downshift) rather than a phase-based programming rule.

### Gaps
- No sourced evidence retrieved on whether asking about occupation (desk/sedentary), motivation style, or dislikes changes training outcomes (adherence literature was not reached). Suggest the writer treat these as adherence/UX inputs.
- Full ACOG 804 absolute/relative contraindication lists, warning signs to stop, activities to avoid (contact sports, scuba, hot yoga, prolonged supine after first trimester) and postpartum timing were not retrievable (acog.org and opqic.org blocked).
- Postpartum guidance (ACOG: resume gradually once medically safe; pelvic floor training) not verified this session.
- Exercise-and-menopause position statements (e.g., British Menopause Society, Exercise & Sports Science Australia) not reached.

## 3. At-home measurements and fitness tests with public normative tables

### Takeaway
A valid no-professional battery exists: anthropometrics (weight, height, waist → WHtR with 0.5 cutoff), resting HR, push-up test (ACSM/CSEP norms), 30-s chair stand (Rikli & Jones norms, older adults), forearm plank (Strand et al. college norms), single-leg stance (Springer 2007 norms by decade), sit-and-reach (ACSM/CSEP norms), knee-to-wall dorsiflexion (cm cutoffs), overhead squat screen (NASM compensation checklist), and a cardio field test (YMCA 3-min step, Cooper 12-min, or Rockport 1-mile walk with a VO2max equation). Wall-sit norms are not from a peer-reviewed source.

### Cited Findings

**Body measurements**
- Waist-to-height ratio: Ashwell et al. 2012 systematic review/meta-analysis of 31 studies, >300,000 adults across ethnic groups: WHtR improved discrimination of cardiometabolic risk (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CVD) by 4–5% over BMI (p<0.01) and was significantly better than waist circumference for diabetes, hypertension and CVD in both sexes (p<0.005). A WHtR of 0.5 is the established cutoff ("keep your waist to less than half your height"); WHtR ≥0.5 identified increased risk with sensitivity 70–80% and specificity 70–75% — [Medical News Today on Ashwell 2012](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/245352/); [Metabolicum summary of Ashwell 2012](https://www.metabolicum.com/learn/research/whtr-ashwell-2012); [Metabolicum on Lee 2008 cutoff validation](https://www.metabolicum.com/learn/research/whtr-lee-2008); [Essex repository 2023 WHtR paper](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/35892/1/orcp2023.pdf)
- Resting heart rate: normal adult range 60–100 bpm; well-trained people may be below 60 and down to ~40 bpm; women show several more bpm than men at each age/fitness level — [AHA target heart rates](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/fitness/fitness-basics/target-heart-rates); [UPMC: normal heart rate](https://share.upmc.com/2015/07/what-is-a-normal-heart-rate/); [Vitruve: RHR in athletes by age/gender](https://vitruve.fit/blog/frequency-of-heart-rate-at-rest-in-athletes-according-to-age-and-gender/)

**Push-up test (ACSM-based norms; men standard, women typically modified/knee)**
- Male standard push-ups, "Average" band: 17–19 y 19–34; 20–29 y 17–29; 30–39 y 13–24; 40–49 y 11–20; 50–59 y 9–17; 60–65 y 6–16. "Excellent": >56, >47, >41, >34, >31, >30 respectively. "Poor": 4–10, 4–9, 2–7, 1–5, 1–4, 1–2 — [Topend Sports push-up norms](https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/push-up.htm); [Sensai: push-up norms by age/sex](https://www.sensai.fit/blog/push-up-test-norms-by-age-sex)
- Female standard push-ups, "Average": 17–19 y 7–10; 20–29 y 9–13; 30–39 y 7–12; 40–49 y 5–9; 50–59 y 4–8; 60–65 y 3–5. "Excellent": >30, >32, >28, >20, >16, >12 — same sources.
- Female modified (knee) push-ups, "Average": 17–19 y 11–20; 20–29 y 12–22; 30–39 y 10–21; 40–49 y 8–17; 50–59 y 7–14; 60–65 y 5–12. "Excellent": >35, >36, >37, >31, >25, >23 — [Topend Sports home push-up test](https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/home-pushup.htm)
- A 2025 open-access paper (PMC12408081) publishes push-up and sit-and-reach normative tables — [PMC12408081 table 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408081/table/db_ds_table-wrap6_reqid_); [table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408081/table/db_ds_table-wrap5_reqid_) (content not retrievable; writer should check it as a citable primary source).

**30-second chair stand (Senior Fitness Test, Rikli & Jones 1999 / Jones & Rikli 2002)**
- "Average" (normal range) reps, men: 60–64 y 14–19; 65–69 y 12–18; 70–74 y 12–17; 75–79 y 11–17; 80–84 y 10–15; 85–89 y 8–14; 90–94 y 7–12. Women: 60–64 y 12–17; 65–69 y 11–16; 70–74 y 10–15; 75–79 y 10–15; 80–84 y 9–14; 85–89 y 8–13; 90–94 y 4–11 — [Topend Sports chair stand calculator (Jones & Rikli 2002 data)](https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/chair-stand.htm); [bpac NZ 30-second chair stand PDF](https://bpac.org.nz/falls/docs/The_30-Second_Chair_Stand_Test.pdf)

**Forearm plank**
- Strand et al., "Fitness norms for the plank exercise" (Int J Exerc Sci: Conf Proc 8(2):14): mean hold 106.15 ± 49.52 s (females) and 117.66 ± 53.49 s (males); female quartiles 73.5 / 95 / 122.5 s (25th/50th/75th); male quartiles 84 / 110 / 135 s — [WKU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol8/iss2/14); [Linfield symposium record](https://digitalcommons.linfield.edu/symposium/2014/all/8) (sample was college-age; see Gaps)

**Knee-to-wall (weight-bearing lunge) ankle dorsiflexion**
- ≥10 cm toe-to-wall = good dorsiflexion; <5 cm = restricted; ≥1.5 cm left-right difference = clinically meaningful asymmetry; ~3.6° per cm; intra-rater ICC >0.97; MDC ≈1.4 cm — [Pabau: knee-to-wall test](https://pabau.com/blog/knee-to-wall-test/); [Carepatron knee-to-wall PDF](https://www.carepatron.com/files/knee-to-wall-test.pdf); [Physiotutors weight-bearing lunge wiki](https://www.physiotutors.com/wiki/weight-bearing-lunge-test); [Prehab Guys assessment](https://library.theprehabguys.com/vimeo-video/knee-to-wall-ankle-dorsiflexion-assessment)

**Overhead squat screen (NASM OSA)**
- Compensations and interpretations: knees move inward (valgus) → lack of ankle ROM and/or hip weakness; overactive adductors/TFL, underactive gluteus medius; valgus is linked to higher knee injury risk. Excessive forward lean → tight calves, hip flexors, lats vs weak core/scapular stabilizers; if lean and arms-forward clean up with heels elevated, ankles are the primary driver. Arms fall forward → short latissimus dorsi (upper-body or LPHC dysfunction) — [NASM blog: how to perform an OSA](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/how-to-perform-an-overhead-squat-assessment-osa); [PT Pioneer NASM OSA](https://www.ptpioneer.com/nasm-overhead-squat-assessment/); [NFPT overhead squat](https://www.nfpt.com/?p=45625)

- Cooper 12-minute run norms (metres). **Wall sit (not peer-reviewed norms)**
- Popular-press benchmarks only: average 20–40 s; women 30–39 y target 60 s; 40–49 y 45–60 s; 50–59 y 30–45 s; 60+ y 20–30 s; over-50 bands <30 s needs work, 30–45 respectable, 45–60 solid, ≥60 s "stronger than 90% of peers" — [BoxRox wall sit women 30+](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=249527); [Eat This wall sit after 50](https://www.eatthis.com/wall-sit-test-after-50-lower-body-strength/). Treat as unvalidated.

### Inferences
- Scoring scheme: convert each test to a 1–5 band (Poor→Excellent) using the age/sex table; use the lowest-scoring domain(s) to weight the program (e.g., push-up band ≤2 → +1 pressing exercise/week; chair-stand below range → lower-body strength + balance emphasis; SLS <10 s eyes open in a 65+ user → fall-risk flag + balance block every session; knee-to-wall <5 cm → ankle mobility block before squats and heel-elevated squat variant; OSA valgus → glute-med work, hip-dominant substitutions).
- For users under ~60, the chair stand will ceiling; use push-up, plank and wall sit for strength-endurance and only use chair stand for 60+.
- Photos have no normative value; use them only for user-facing progress, never for scoring.

- Plank norms above are from a college-age sample (Strand et al.); no age-stratified peer-reviewed plank norms found. The "60–120 s for ages 18–29" style figures circulating online are from fitness media, not studies.
- Apley scratch test interpretation/norms, resting-HR fitness bands by age (the Topend/NIH-style table), ACSM waist-circumference and BMI risk cutoffs, and the CSEP-PATH norms were not retrieved before the search budget ended.

## 4. Program-design rules (WHO 2020, ACSM FITT-VP, NSCA load/rep/rest, volume, frequency, periodization, deloads, RPE/RIR, splits, minimum dose)

### Takeaway
The quantitative rule set is well established: WHO 150–300 min moderate (or 75–150 vigorous) aerobic + muscle-strengthening; NSCA load/rep/rest bands by goal; hypertrophy volume dose-response up to ≥10 sets/muscle/week with ≥2 sessions/muscle/week as the default; linear vs daily-undulating periodization produce similar results so pick by adherence; novices progress load 2–10% when they exceed target reps by 1–2; RIR-anchored RPE regulates load day to day; a minimum effective dose of ~1 hard set per exercise per session can still raise 1RM in trained men.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO 2020:** all adults should do 150–300 min moderate-intensity or 75–150 min vigorous-intensity aerobic activity (or equivalent combination) per week; regular muscle-strengthening is recommended for all age groups; children/adolescents average 60 min/day MVPA; sedentary time should be reduced but no threshold could be quantified; new recommendations cover pregnant/postpartum women and people with chronic conditions/disability — [Bull et al. 2020, BJSM (Cambridge repository)](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/312925); [UEA record](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/77844)
- **NSCA load/rep/rest by goal:** Strength ≥85% 1RM, 1–6 reps, 2–6 sets, 2–5 min rest; Power single-effort 80–90%, 1–2 reps, 3–5 sets, 2–5 min; Power multiple-effort 75–85%, 3–5 reps, 3–5 sets, 2–5 min; Hypertrophy 67–85%, 6–12 reps, 3–6 sets, 30–90 s; Muscular endurance ≤67%, ≥12 reps, 2–3 sets, ≤30 s — [PT Pioneer CSCS ch.17](https://www.ptpioneer.com/personal-training/certifications/nsca-cscs/cscs-chapter-17/); [Achievable NSCA load chart](https://achievable.me/define/nsca-load-chart/); [Movement System CSCS program design](https://www.themovementsystem.com/blog/cscs-program-design)
- **ACSM FITT-VP for novices:** 60–70% 1RM (8–12 RM), 1–3 sets, 2–3 d/wk, single- and multi-joint exercises; progression: increase load 2–10% once the person can perform 1–2 reps above target on the current load — [SFU ACSM resistance PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf); [ACSM 2009 Position Stand](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf); [ExRx guidelines](https://exrx.net/WeightTraining/Guidelines.html)
- **Weekly volume dose-response:** Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (15 studies): graded dose-response with hypertrophy increasing from 1–4 → 5–9 → 10+ sets/muscle/week; insufficient data above ~10 sets; only two studies used trained subjects — [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=56687); [Biolayne "king of volume metas"](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-31/the-king-of-volume-metas/); [Strength Framework](https://strengthframework.beehiiv.com/p/how-much-set-volume-for-hypertrophy)
- **Frequency:** Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016 (Sports Medicine, 10 studies): ≥2 sessions/muscle/week produced greater hypertrophy than 1; insufficient evidence for >2; most studies were not volume-equated so frequency and volume are confounded; "at least twice a week" is the sensible default — [Stronger by Science frequency](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/frequency/); [Built With Science](https://builtwithscience.com/fitness-tips/how-often-to-train/)
- **Periodization:** Grgic et al. 2017 (PeerJ 5:e3695), 13 studies: LP vs DUP pooled SMD for hypertrophy −0.02 (95% CI −0.25 to 0.21, p = 0.848) → effects likely similar — [PeerJ 3695](https://peerj.com/articles/3695); [PMC5571788](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5571788); [Stronger by Science research spotlight](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-periodization-style/)
- **Deloads:** Bell, Strafford, Coleman, Korakakis & Nolan, Sports Medicine – Open 2023, international Delphi (34 → 29 → 21 expert coaches over 3 rounds) defined a deload as "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for the subsequent training cycle"; round 1 covered 15 open questions across general perceptions, applications, design/implementation, and inclusive environment — [PMC10809978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/); [SHURA PDF](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417/1/s40798-023-00633-0.pdf); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/599784d71dff41b197ff5cf66a3d11dd)
- **RPE/RIR autoregulation:** Helms et al. (Strength Cond J 2016; Frontiers in Physiology 2018) RIR-based RPE scale: each scale value is defined by perceived reps in reserve; traditional Borg RPE yields submaximal scores (6.8–9.0) even at failure, whereas trainees report RIR more reliably; RIR-RPE correlated inversely with bar velocity in squat (r = −0.87) and bench (r = −0.79); practical use is to regulate daily load and to guide 1RM testing — [Frontiers in Physiology 2018 (Helms et al.)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/epub); [AUT repository: Novel RT-specific RPE scale](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/efef3b25-6701-4fb5-bb82-55fcd2a26027/full); [JOHK: Methods for regulating/monitoring RT](https://johk.pl/?p=5643)
- **Minimum effective dose:** Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021 (Frontiers Sports Act Living): for powerlifters, ~3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps/week per lift, spread over 1–3 sessions, loads >80% 1RM at RPE 7.5–9.5 for 6–12 weeks produce strength gains; autoregulated single-rep sets at RPE 9–9.5 are the floor (gains less likely meaningful) and adding 2–3 back-off sets at ~80% of the single improves gains; an associated meta-analysis found 1 set per exercise per session sufficient to raise 1RM across 6 studies in trained males — [Frontiers full text](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/text); [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-minimum-effective-training-dose-required-for-1rm-strength-in-/)

### Inferences (encodable rule set)
- Weekly set targets per major muscle group (chest, back, quads, hamstrings/glutes, shoulders, core): novice 6–10, intermediate 10–16, advanced 12–20; never allocate <2 touches/week per muscle when days ≥3. Rationale: dose-response to ≥10 sets with frequency ≥2 as default; cap derived from the fact that evidence above ~10–20 sets is thin.
- Splits by days/week (standard practice consistent with the ≥2 frequency rule): 2 d → full body ×2; 3 d → full body ×3; 4 d → upper/lower ×2; 5 d → upper/lower/full or push/pull/legs + upper/lower; 6 d → push/pull/legs ×2. For home users with limited equipment, full-body is preferred at 2–4 days because set counts per muscle are small and sessions short.
- Rep/intensity mapping for bodyweight/band work (loads cannot be set as %1RM): use RIR targets instead — strength emphasis RIR 1–3 at hard variations (≤8 reps possible); hypertrophy RIR 1–3 in the 6–20 rep window; endurance RIR 2–4 at 15–30 reps. Novices overestimate RIR, so clamp their effective RIR 1 unit lower in the engine and use "reps achieved vs target" as the main progress signal.
- Progression (double progression for bodyweight/bands): when all sets hit the top of the rep window at RIR ≥2 for two consecutive sessions → progress to the next variation (leverage/ROM/tempo/unilateral) or add a set; when the user fails the bottom of the window twice → regress a variation. Load-based analogue is ACSM's 2–10% rule.
- Periodization for novices/intermediates: since LP ≈ DUP, use simple linear progression blocks of 4–6 weeks (3–5 loading weeks + 1 deload week), with undulation within the week only if the user has ≥3 sessions and reports boredom; adherence/preference should break the tie.
- Deload: default every 4–8 weeks (proactive) or when triggered (reactive: RPE creep ≥1 above target for 2 weeks, performance drop, persistent soreness, sleep/stress flags). *Exact magnitudes (commonly ~50% volume reduction, keep intensity) could not be verified against the Delphi text; see Gaps.*

### Gaps
- Bell et al. 2023 Delphi: specific consensus numbers (deload every 4–6 weeks, ~1 week long, reduce volume ~50% while keeping intensity) not verified — PMC/SHURA blocked.
- Helms RIR scale exact anchors (10 = 0 RIR, 9.5 = could add load but not reps, 9 = 1 RIR … 5.5) not retrieved from the Frontiers page.

## 5. Yoga sequencing logic and Pilates mat progression logic

### Takeaway
Encodable yoga rules center on building component actions toward a peak pose, moving through pose families in a heating-to-cooling arc with counterposes, and placing pranayama/meditation at the ends (Mark Stephens' five principles and seven asana families). Classical Pilates mat work is a fixed 34-exercise order from Joseph Pilates' *Return to Life* (Hundred → Push-Ups), designed as one continuous exercise where each elementary move contains the building blocks of later ones; levels are layered by omitting advanced exercises and modifying.

### Cited Findings
- Mark Stephens, *Yoga Sequencing: Designing Transformative Yoga Classes* (North Atlantic Books, 2012) presents the essential principles and methods for planning and sequencing classes; the companion Yoga Sequencing Deck includes a booklet covering **five principles of sequencing** and 100 cards color-coded by pose family; the book explores interrelationships "within and between the seven asana families" and the anatomy of opening and stabilizing each pose for sequences designed around specific needs — [North Atlantic Books: Yoga Sequencing Deck](https://www.northatlanticbooks.com/?p=42533); [Goodreads: Yoga Sequencing](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/13330396)
- Peak-pose logic: practice "component actions," often in increasing level of difficulty, until arriving at the pose of greatest complexity in which all component actions come together — [ACE: The art of intelligently sequencing yoga classes](https://www.acefitness.org/education-and-resources/professional/prosource/september-2016/6034/the-art-of-intelligently-sequencing-yoga-classes)
- Effective sequencing lets practitioners flow from active to passive, heating to cooling poses without strain — [BeYogi: Yoga sequencing for success](https://beyogi.com/yoga-sequencing-for-success/); seven alternative sequencing frameworks are summarized by Yoga International — [Yoga International: 7 ways to sequence](https://yogainternational.com/article/view/7-ways-to-sequence-a-yoga-practice)
- Classical Pilates: the 34 exercises in Joseph Pilates' *Return to Life Through Contrology* are the foundation of classical Pilates; he was adamant they be performed in the exact order; the mat work is viewed as one long exercise evolving from the Hundred to Push-Ups; the work "builds and layers concepts and skills as the exercises increase in difficulty" and "each elementary exercise contains the building blocks of the more advanced work" — [Alo Moves: original 34 Pilates exercises](https://blog.alomoves.com/movement/original-34-pilates-exercises); [Pilates Bridge: from the Hundred to Push Up](https://pilatesbridge.com/pilates-order); [Online Pilates Classes: 34 mat sequence](https://onlinepilatesclasses.com/blog/the-original-34-classical-pilates-mat-exercises); [Pilatesology](https://pilatesology.com/?p=323021); [Pilates Anytime quiz](https://www.pilatesanytime.com/blog/mat/how-well-do-you-know-your-pilates-mat-exercises)
- Order as captured from search summaries (partial): 1 Hundred, 2 Roll Up, 3 Roll Over, … 10 Corkscrew, 11 Saw, 12 Swan Dive, 13 Single Leg Kick, 14 Double Leg Kick, 15 Neck Pull, 16 Scissors, 17 Bicycle, 18 Shoulder Bridge, 19 Spine Twist, 20 Jackknife, 21 Side Kicks, 22 Teaser, 23 Hip Twist/Circles, 24 Swimming, 25 Leg Pull Front, 26 Leg Pull (Back), 27–28 Side Kick Kneeling / Side Bend, 29 Boomerang, 30 Seal, 31 Crab, 32 Rocking, 33 Control Balance, 34 Push-Ups — [Pilates Bridge order](https://pilatesbridge.com/pilates-order) (numbering 4–9 and 23/27–28 not confirmed; see Gaps)

### Inferences (encodable rules)
- Yoga session template (consistent with the arc principle): centering/breath (5%) → gentle warm-up / sun-salutation-type integration (15–20%) → standing poses building the component actions of the day's peak (30%) → peak pose family (15%) → counterposes/neutralizing (10%) → seated/supine cooling, forward folds, twists (15%) → pranayama + savasana (10%). Rule: never follow deep backbends directly with deep forward folds; insert a neutral (child's pose, gentle twist) first; place breath-retention pranayama only after asana, not before vigorous flow.
- Pose-family ordering heuristic: standing → balance → inversions/arm balances (if appropriate) → backbends → twists → forward bends → restorative. Component-action prerequisite graph: e.g., peak = Wheel requires {hip-flexor length, thoracic extension, shoulder flexion, quad strength}; feed from Low Lunge, Cobra/Sphinx, Puppy pose, Bridge.
- Pilates levels: beginner = Hundred, Roll Up (modified), Single Leg Circle, Rolling Like a Ball, Single/Double Leg Stretch, Spine Stretch Forward, Saw, Swan prep, Side Kicks, Seal; intermediate adds Roll Over, Corkscrew, Single/Double Leg Kick, Neck Pull, Shoulder Bridge, Spine Twist, Teaser prep, Swimming, Leg Pull Front; advanced adds Scissors, Bicycle, Jackknife, full Teaser, Leg Pull Back, Side Bend, Boomerang, Crab, Rocking, Control Balance, Push-Ups. *This tiering is standard studio practice, not sourced this session.*
- Pilates substitution rules: acute low-back pain or osteoporosis → remove loaded spinal flexion (Roll Over, Jackknife, Neck Pull, Crab) and keep neutral-spine/extension work (Swimming, Swan prep, Shoulder Bridge); pregnancy → remove supine work after first trimester and prone work, keep side-lying series.

## 6. Mobility/flexibility, balance for older adults, low back pain (McGill Big 3), posture, pelvic floor, pregnancy/postpartum, menopause, knee/shoulder modifications

### Takeaway
Flexibility: weekly total time matters more than per-session time — ≥5 min/week per muscle group, static stretching, ≥5 days/week. Balance for older adults: programs that challenge balance and total >3 h/week cut falls the most (~39%). Low back: McGill Big 3 with descending-pyramid isometric holds. Menopause: heavy resistance (70–85% 1RM) plus impact for bone. Pregnancy: 150 min/week moderate per ACOG. Knee/shoulder-specific protocols and pelvic floor dosing could not be sourced before the budget ran out.

### Cited Findings
- **Flexibility dose:** Thomas, Bianco, Paoli & Palma 2018 (Int J Sports Med 39(4):243–254): time spent stretching per week is fundamental when ≥5 min/week per muscle; time within a single session did not significantly affect ROM gains; weekly frequency is positively associated with ROM; all stretching types improve ROM long-term but static protocols showed significantly greater gains (p<0.05) than ballistic or PNF; recommendation: static stretching ≥5 days/week, ≥5 min/week — [PubMed 29506306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/); [UniPa IRIS record](https://iris.unipa.it/handle/10447/293353); acute-effects meta-analysis of stretching techniques (2023) — [PubMed 37962709](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37962709/)
- **Falls/balance (Sherrington et al. 2019 Cochrane; 108 RCTs, 23,407 participants, 25 countries):** exercise reduced rate of falls in community-dwelling older people by 21–23%; greater effects from programs that challenge balance and involve >3 h/week; these two variables explained 76% of between-trial heterogeneity and together gave a 39% reduction; balance/functional training alone −24%; balance plus resistance −34% — [McMaster Optimal Aging summary](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/0951cd680456e3abeddfd10a82d8ab41); [George Institute release](https://www.georgeinstitute.org.au/media-releases/new-study-shows-how-to-stay-on-your-feet-in-old-age); [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/exercise-to-prevent-falls-in-older-adults-an-updated-systematic-r/); [BJSM 2017 systematic review PDF](https://bjsm-bmj-com.bibliotheek.ehb.be/content/bjsports/51/24/1750.full.pdf)
- **McGill Big 3 (curl-up, side plank, bird dog):** descending pyramid — curl-up 6/4/2 reps with 8–10 s holds; side plank held up to 10 s, repeat ×5 each side; bird dog 6/4/2 per side, hold 1 s building to 3 s; pause 3–5 s between reps — [Harvard Health: Three moves for better spine health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/three-moves-for-better-spine-health); [MUSC Health: the Big Three](https://muschealth.org/content-hub/News/2022/12/20/the-big-three); [Oxygen Mag](https://www.oxygenmag.com/workouts-for-women/ab-workouts-for-women/the-mcgill-big-3-for-core-stability/?scope=anon)
- **Menopause/bone:** ~70–85% 1RM, ~3 sessions/week, ≥6–12 months for BMD; resistance + impact strongest spinal osteogenic stimulus (LIFTMOR; STOP-EM 80–85% 1RM twice weekly + drop landings) — [RSD Journal review](https://rsdjournal.org/rsd/article/view/50935); [Medznat STOP-EM summary](https://www.medznat.ru/en/science/summaries/exercise-for-bone-and-muscle-health-during-menopau); [Osteoporosis Int. (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00198-012-2143-2)
- **Pregnancy:** ACOG CO 804 — ≥150 min/week moderate aerobic; continue prior vigorous activity if habitually active; modifications for anatomic/physiologic changes — [OPQIC summary](https://opqic.org/acog-committee-opinion-no-804-physical-activity-and-exercise-during-pregnancy-and-the-postpartum-period/)
- **Knee valgus / corrective logic (NASM):** knee valgus on OSA → strengthen gluteus medius, lengthen adductors/TFL, address ankle dorsiflexion — [NASM OSA blog](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/how-to-perform-an-overhead-squat-assessment-osa); dorsiflexion <5 cm knee-to-wall is a restriction flag — [Pabau knee-to-wall](https://pabau.com/blog/knee-to-wall-test/)

### Inferences
- Mobility prescription rule: for each flagged tight region, schedule 5 × 60 s/week minimum (e.g., 2 × 30 s on 5 days) static holds, placed at session end or on rest days; dynamic mobility only in warm-ups. Progress to 2 × 60 s if the test band is "Poor".
- Older-adult (65+) template: every session begins with a balance block (static → semi-tandem → tandem → single-leg → eyes-closed/foam progression), total weekly movement ≥3 h including walking, plus 2×/week lower-body strength (sit-to-stand progressions). Fall-risk flag if SLS <10 s or chair stand below the Rikli & Jones range.
- Low-back-pain path (non-specific, cleared): McGill Big 3 daily or 5×/week for 4–6 weeks; remove loaded spinal flexion and end-range extension; keep hip hinge pattern with neutral spine; reintroduce flexion-based Pilates gradually.
- Desk-worker path: pair the Big 3 with thoracic extension, hip-flexor stretch (5 min/week rule), and scapular/neck strengthening; break sitting every 30–60 min (WHO: reduce sedentary time; no threshold).
- Pregnancy path: moderate intensity (talk test), avoid supine positions after first trimester and prone loading, avoid impact/contact and hot environments, include pelvic floor training; hard-stop and refer on any warning sign.
- Menopause path: when goal includes bone/body composition and the user has dumbbells/kettlebell and no contraindication: 2–3×/week heavy compound work progressing toward RIR 2–3 at ≤8 reps, plus low-level impact (hops/heel drops) if joints tolerate; otherwise band/bodyweight strength with step-ups and hops.

## 7. Adaptation logic from session feedback (RPE, completion, pain, soreness, sleep) and weight-trend rules

### Takeaway
The literature supports RIR-anchored RPE as the per-set autoregulation signal (with the caveat that novices are less accurate), a progression trigger based on exceeding target reps (ACSM 2–10% rule), stress/sleep as recovery modifiers, and a weekly weight-loss rate target of 0.5–1% body weight for calorie adjustments. Specific thresholds such as "RPE ≤6 for 2 sessions → progress" are practitioner conventions, not studied cutoffs.

### Cited Findings
- RIR-based RPE is a practical method to regulate daily training load; it tracks bar velocity closely (squat r = −0.87, bench r = −0.79); Borg RPE is unreliable near failure — [Frontiers Physiology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/epub); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/efef3b25-6701-4fb5-bb82-55fcd2a26027/full)
- ACSM progression trigger: increase load 2–10% when the trainee performs the current workload for 1–2 reps over the target on consecutive sessions — [SFU ACSM resistance PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)
- Chronic psychological stress slows recovery of muscular function over 96 h and high life stress blunts 12-week strength gains — [PubMed 24343323](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24343323/); [NASE summary of Bartholomew 2008](https://www.naseinc.com/blog/effect-of-perceived-stress-on-strength-gains/)
- Sleep restriction mainly degrades strength-endurance/volume tolerance rather than 1RM — [Stronger by Science research spotlight](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-sleep-loss/)
- Deload definition and purpose (reduce fatigue, enhance preparedness for the next cycle) — [PMC10809978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/)
- Rate of weight loss: Helms, Aragon & Fitschen 2014 (JISSN) recommend 0.5–1% body weight per week to preserve muscle; Garthe et al. 2011 — slow (0.7%/wk) vs fast (1.4%/wk) loss in athletes: both lost ~5.5% body weight, slow group gained 2.1% lean mass, fast group none — [MacroFactor cutting calculator article](https://macrofactor.com/cutting-calculator/); [Barbell Medicine forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/is-there-a-limit-for-healthy-weight-loss-rate/9177)

### Inferences (proposed rule table; conventions, not sourced thresholds)
- Per-exercise progression: `if reps ≥ top_of_window on all sets AND reported RIR ≥ 2 (RPE ≤ 8) for 2 consecutive sessions → progress (next variation / +1 set / +2–10% load)`.
- Hold: `if reps within window and RPE 8–9 → keep`.
- Regress: `if reps < bottom_of_window on ≥2 sets OR RPE 10 with form breakdown → regress variation or −1 set`.
- Pain: `if pain flag ≥3/10 during an exercise → substitute same-pattern, lower-ROM or isometric variant next session; if pain flag ≥3/10 on the same region 3 sessions in a row, or any pain ≥7/10, or pain with numbness/tingling/night pain → remove pattern and show refer-to-clinician message`.
- Soreness: `if soreness ≥7/10 in a muscle at its next scheduled session → cut that muscle's sets by ~⅓ this session; if it persists two weeks → trigger deload`.
- Skips: `if ≥50% of sessions skipped in a week → reduce planned days by one and shorten sessions before re-expanding; if "too long" is cited → cut accessory sets first`.
- Sleep/stress: `if sleep <6 h or stress "high" logged for ≥3 of 7 days → next week −1 set per exercise and RIR target +1` (supported directionally by the stress/sleep findings above).
- Deload: `every 4–8 weeks proactively (shorter for novices on fast progression? no — longer, since novices accumulate less fatigue) or reactively when: average session RPE ≥1 point above target for 2 weeks, performance drops ≥2 sessions, or soreness/sleep triggers fire; deload week = ~½ sets, same exercises, RIR 3–4`.
- Weight-trend calorie rule: use a 7-day rolling average; require ≥14 days (two weekly averages) of ≥4 weigh-ins per week before adjusting; if weekly change is outside the 0.5–1%/wk target band for fat loss (or 0.25–0.5%/wk gain for lean gain), adjust intake by ~100–200 kcal/day; never adjust on a single week during menstruation-related water shifts.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source retrieved for soreness- or RPE-threshold-based week-to-week volume adjustment (Renaissance Periodization MEV/MAV/MRV and Barbell Medicine autoregulation articles were not reached). Present these as practitioner heuristics.
- Primary JISSN citation page for Helms 2014 and Garthe 2011 (Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab) not opened; figures come from secondary summaries.
- Minimum data for weight-trend adjustment (e.g., MacroFactor's 1–2 week cadence) not verified.

## 8. Nutrition calculation basics (Mifflin-St Jeor, activity factors, protein, deficit/surplus, fiber, hydration, special cases)

### Takeaway
Mifflin-St Jeor is the most accurate common RMR equation; TDEE = RMR × 1.2–1.9; protein 1.6 g/kg/day is the evidence breakpoint for lean-mass gains (upper range ~2.2 for safety margin); fat-loss deficit sized to 0.5–1% body weight per week. Fiber/hydration references and the rules for refusing deficits in minors/pregnancy were not sourced before the budget ran out.

### Cited Findings
- Mifflin-St Jeor: men RMR = 10×kg + 6.25×cm − 5×age + 5; women = 10×kg + 6.25×cm − 5×age − 161; activity multipliers sedentary 1.2, lightly active 1.375, moderately active 1.55, active 1.725, very active 1.9; a comparison of four equations found Mifflin-St Jeor most likely to predict RMR within 10% of measured — [QxMD Mifflin-St Jeor](https://calculate.qxmd.com/storage/calculators/846/Mifflin-StJeorEquation.html); [MedicineNet: which calorie calculator is most accurate](https://www.medicinenet.com/which_calorie_calculator_is_most_accurate/article.htm); [Inch Calculator](https://inchcalculator.com/mifflin-st-jeor-calculator)
- Protein: Morton et al. 2018 (BJSM 52(6):376), 49 studies, 1,863 participants, ≥6 weeks RT, no intentional energy restriction: protein supplementation significantly increased strength, fat-free mass and fiber CSA; biphasic regression breakpoint — no further FFM gain beyond total intake of 1.62 g/kg/day; effect smaller with age, larger in trained individuals — [BJSM abstract](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376.abstract); [PubMed 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/); [Stronger by Science commentary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61316)
- Deficit size via rate: 0.5–1% body weight/week (Helms 2014); slow loss preserved/gained lean mass vs fast loss (Garthe 2011) — [MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/cutting-calculator/)
- WHO 2020 includes recommendations for pregnant/postpartum women (activity, not diet) — [Bull et al. 2020](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/312925)

### Inferences

## 9. Safety, legal claims, hard-stops, eating-disorder screening (SCOFF)

### Takeaway
The SCOFF is a 5-item screen (score ≥2 = likely eating disorder; sensitivity ~100%, specificity ~87.5%, 12.5% false positives) suitable as an in-app gate before any weight-loss content. Hard-stops should mirror PAR-Q+ "yes" answers plus ACSM signs/symptoms and ACOG pregnancy warning signs. No legal/claims guidance source was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- SCOFF (Morgan, Reid & Lacey 1999) five questions: (S) "Do you make yourself Sick because you feel uncomfortably full?"; (C) "Do you worry that you have lost Control over how much you eat?"; (O) "Have you recently lost more than One stone (≈14 lb / 6.35 kg) in a three-month period?"; (F) "Do you believe yourself to be Fat when others say you are too thin?"; (F) "Would you say that Food dominates your life?" Scoring: 1 point per yes; ≥2 indicates a likely case of anorexia or bulimia; sensitivity 100%, specificity 87.5% in the original study; not to be used alone for diagnosis — [Children's Mercy SCOFF PDF](https://www.cmh.edu/siteassets/media-documents-for-depts-section/documents-for-health-care-providers/block-clinical-practice-guidelines/scoff-questionnaire-for-childrens-mercy.pdf); [NHS Penybont SCOFF PDF](https://www.penybontsurgery.wales.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/518/2021/01/SCOFF.pdf); [InsideOut Institute SCOFF](https://insideoutinstitute.org.au/assets/scoff-questionnaire.pdf); [Alliance for Eating Disorders: history/efficacy](https://www.allianceforeatingdisorders.com/what-is-the-history-and-efficacy-of-the-scoff-screening-tool/); [openEHR SCOFF archetype](https://ckm.openehr.org/ckm/archetypes/1013.1.3719)
- PAR-Q+ "yes" → follow-up → ePARmed-X+/physician before becoming more active — [HealthLinkBC](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/node/11457)
- ACSM 2015: signs/symptoms of CV/metabolic/renal disease trigger medical clearance regardless of exercise history — [ACE summary](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/6921/new-preparticipation-screening-guidelines-what-health-and-fitness-pros-need-to-know/)
- WHtR ≥0.5 = "increased health risk requiring lifestyle intervention" (self-screening message) — [Metabolicum/Ashwell](https://www.metabolicum.com/learn/research/whtr-ashwell-2012)
